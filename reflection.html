Reflective Introduction

From describing food to understanding how writing makes meaning.

Journey Overview Evidence Gallery Final Drafts

When I entered WR40, I thought of food writing as something personal and descriptive. If someone asked me to write about food, I expected to talk about whether it tasted good, whether I liked it, or whether it reminded me of a particular moment. I did not think carefully about the difference between describing food and constructing an experience for a reader. This portfolio shows how that understanding changed. The pieces I selected are not arranged by week because my growth did not feel like a simple calendar. Instead, I organized the portfolio as a movement from before, to during, to after: from instinctive personal response, to analysis of technique, to more intentional revision of my own writing.

The first artifact I want readers to notice is my first-day writing about Oreo as the worst food. On the surface, it is a simple preference response. But when I look back now, I can see that I was already writing about a larger idea: food is connected to trust, memory, fear, and the body. I wrote that Oreo became uncomfortable for me after I associated it with acute gastroenteritis, even though I knew logically that the cookie was probably not the cause. At the time, I did not fully understand why this mattered as writing. Now I see it as my first step toward realizing that food is never only food in a text. It can become evidence of emotion, culture, identity, and vulnerability.

Annotation was the part of the course that most changed the way I read. Before this class, I often treated annotation as marking important ideas so I could remember them later. In WR40, I learned to annotate for method. In my Tasker annotations, I paid attention to phrases like "slim and pale," "mournful pillow-soft sleeve," and "methodically distributed." Those small choices made the burrito feel strange, fragile, engineered, and almost threatening. The point was not simply that Tasker was being funny. The point was that his humor came from technique: horror language, contrast, exaggeration, and a tone that treated fast food as if it were a serious danger. This helped me understand that analysis should explain how meaning is built, not just what meaning exists.

Wendig's apple ranking pushed this even further for me. His writing showed that voice can be a kind of argument. He is not only ranking apples; he is creating a speaker who is dramatic, judgmental, self-aware, and funny. In my annotations, I noticed the mock-epic title, the absurd comparison to public hearings, and the insult that turns an apple into a pop-culture judgment. Reading Wendig made me think differently about my own sentences. I began to ask what kind of speaker my writing created. Was I sounding uncertain? Too general? Too distant from my own ideas? Voice became something I could revise, not something that simply appeared naturally.

The Sides helped me practice these discoveries without the pressure of a major essay. My "Description for Effect" response about the Honey Chicken Super Bowl was especially important because I tried to control mood on purpose. Instead of only listing ingredients, I used repetition, contrast, pacing, and abstract language to make the bowl feel calm, balanced, and almost ceremonial. The peer comment I received was useful because it did not only praise the piece. It also suggested that I could imitate Tasker more strongly by adding more emphasis and metaphor. That feedback helped me understand workshop as a place to see the gap between intention and effect. I might know what I wanted a piece to do, but another reader could show me whether the writing actually did it.

My understanding of revision changed the most during the Food Fight unit. Earlier in the quarter, I thought revision mainly meant improving grammar, changing awkward sentences, or making a draft sound smoother. The Food Fight workshops taught me that revision is also rethinking the structure of an argument. In the body paragraph workshop, I had to identify my best and weakest paragraphs. That activity made me realize that a paragraph can sound connected to the essay while still not doing enough work. A strong paragraph needs a clear job. It should make a claim, use specific evidence, explain that evidence, and return to the larger argument. My weaker paragraph needed more than polishing; it needed sharper evidence and a clearer reason to exist.

The conclusion workshop also changed my revision process. I learned that a conclusion should not simply repeat the introduction in different words. It should show consequence. After the reader has followed the evidence, what should they understand differently? What question should they carry away? For Food Fight, that meant connecting the food debate to larger issues of memory, belonging, value, and who gets to define tradition. This helped me evaluate my progress toward the course goals because I could see myself moving from response to argument. I was no longer only saying what I thought; I was trying to make readers understand why the issue mattered.

This portfolio also shows my growing sense of ownership over my writing process. The cookbook reflections were useful because they forced me to pause and name what I was learning while I was still learning it. In Cookbook 1, I wrote that language can shape the way we experience ordinary food and that description is never neutral. That idea became one of the main threads of my portfolio. Looking back, I can see that many of my later choices came from that early realization. I became more aware that tone, sentence order, evidence, and visual presentation all guide the reader. Writing is not only having an idea; it is designing the path by which someone else can reach that idea.

My goal as a writer is to become more precise and more confident. I still sometimes struggle with making my analysis specific enough, especially when I am writing about large ideas like culture, identity, or tradition. But I have improved at noticing when my writing becomes too general. I now know that I can return to a concrete detail, ask what work the detail is doing, and build analysis from there. I have also learned that revision is not a sign that the first draft failed. Revision is where the real thinking often becomes visible.

If this portfolio has one central claim, it is that I learned to treat writing as a set of choices. Food writing gave me a subject that looked familiar, but the course showed me how much meaning could be hidden inside that familiarity. A cookie could become a story about trust. A burrito review could become satire. An apple ranking could become a performance of voice. A workshop comment could become a plan for revision. By the end of WR40, I was not only collecting assignments. I was learning how to read my own writing as something made, revised, and capable of changing how a reader sees an ordinary thing.
