First-Day Writing: Best/Worst Food Ever

WR40 · First-Day Writing Ann Ge · Mar 30

Best/Worst Food Ever

I think Oreo is the worst food, not because its taste, but because of it shows how fragile our trust in food can be. One time, I ate Oreo and later had acute gastroenteritis. I know this was most likely a coincidence, but the experience permanently changed how I feel about it. Since then, I cannot eat Oreo without feeling uncomfortable, even though I understand logically that it was not the cause.

This experience reveals that our relationship with food is not purely rational. A single negative memory can override logic and reshape our preferences. Many people have similar experiences, such that after getting sick from a certain food, they avoid it for years, even if the food itself was not responsible. In this sense, the "worst" food is not the one that tastes bad, but the one that becomes associated with discomfort and loss of control.

Oreo, for me, represents that moment where trust breaks. It is not just a cookie anymore, but a reminder that something as ordinary as food can suddenly feel unsafe.

My Thinking Process

At first, I thought the prompt was asking me to choose a food based only on taste. But when I started writing about Oreo, I realized my answer was really about memory and emotion. The food itself was ordinary, but the experience around it changed the way I understood it.

This piece helped me see that food writing can move beyond description. It can also show fear, trust, discomfort, and the way one small memory can reshape a person's relationship with something familiar.

Reviews of New Food: Taco Bell's Rattlesnake Burrito

Kevin Tasker, McSweeney's Internet Tendency, May 14, 2019.

Open Original Article

How I Present My Annotation

To avoid copying the full article, this section focuses on my own Perusall comments. I use only short phrases from the original text as evidence, then show how I responded to Tasker's tone, imagery, exaggeration, and satire.

My Thinking Process

This was the first time I felt that a food review could be so unusual. At the beginning, I was not even sure what the author's attitude was. The writing did not simply describe whether the food tasted good or bad; instead, it made the burrito feel strange, dramatic, and almost unsafe.

Reading and annotating this piece helped me understand that food writing does not always have to describe food directly. A writer can write about food through mood, exaggeration, horror language, humor, and surprise. This changed my idea of what a food review could do.

Ann Ge Annotation

Unusual Word Choice

"slim and pale"

The author uses these two words, which are usually used to describe people or dead bodies, to describe the Rattlesnake Burrito, making the food seem abnormal. This might have hinted from the very beginning that this article was not an ordinary food review, but rather a piece of writing with a satirical tone.

Ann Ge Annotation

Harmless Surface, Horror Inside

"mournful pillow-soft sleeve"

This phrase describes the burrito's appearance, creating a sense of harmlessness and even fragility. It is kind of a contrast setup for the following content.

Ann Ge Annotation

Engineered Pain

"methodically distributed"

"Methodically" sounds precise and scientific. It makes the distribution of peppers feel intentional, as if the pain is carefully engineered.

Ann Ge Annotation

Exaggeration as Satire

contrast and horror language

I agree with the point about contrast, but I think it goes beyond creating a horror experience. The exaggeration does not just make the burrito seem scary; it turns something ordinary into something very dramatic. By using language that seems too intense for fast food, Tasker makes the review funny and pokes fun at fast food itself.

Ann Ge Annotation

Relief That Fails

"were merely jesting!"

I like the idea that relief is being denied here. The mock-religious tone makes the experience feel like a failed ritual rather than just eating food. The "twist" is not really positive because the fries make the experience worse. That irony becomes part of the joke.

Annotation: Wendig

Source Link

In Which I Rank Grocery Store Apples

Chuck Wendig, Terribleminds, January 14, 2019.

Open Original Article

How I Present My Annotation

This section will focus on my Perusall comments on Wendig's apple ranking. I will use short phrases from the original article as evidence, then explain how the author's voice, humor, and ranking style changed the way I thought about food writing.

My Thinking Process

Wendig's writing made me notice how food writing can depend on voice as much as description. He is not only ranking apples; he is also creating a funny speaker who sounds dramatic, picky, and self-aware at the same time.

While annotating, I paid attention to how ordinary apples became connected to larger ideas like politics, consumer culture, access, and popularity. This helped me see that a simple ranking can still have a strong personality and a larger point of view.

Ann Ge Annotation

Mock-Epic Framing

"In Which I..."

Mock-epic. "In which I..." is like the classical essay or chapter titles, creating ironic elegance for a trivial topic.

Ann Ge Annotation

Comic Persona

"SCOTUS judge"

The absurd hypothetical and parody of political hearings help build a comedic persona. The writer treats an "apple problem" as if it were serious enough to require public defense.

Ann Ge Annotation

Contradictory Self-Presentation

"a savage apple snob"

There is a contradiction between "savage" and "snob." He admits he is picky, but he also pretends that he is innocent. This makes the narrator feel funny and self-aware.

Ann Ge Annotation

Clarifying Food Access

"food deserts and grocery stores"

I think there might be a misunderstanding that "food desert" does not mean dessert. The author is not comparing apples to sweets, but pointing out how distribution affects what kinds of apples people can actually get.

Ann Ge Annotation

Popularity and Safety

"Top-40 pop music apple"

I am not sure if he is stating it is slightly bland or criticizing it. Because Ed Sheeran makes people feel emotionally safe, the comparison suggests that Honeycrisp is popular and comfortable, but maybe not very interesting.

Ann Ge Annotation

Direct Insult as Humor

"YOU'RE THE ED SHEERAN OF APPLES"

The ending turns the comparison into a direct insult. It is funny because the language is too intense for an apple, which makes the ranking feel personal and exaggerated.

Side: Description for Effect

You're going to describe a food and try to influence our feelings about that food through the power of your description.

Choose a food, then decide how you want readers to feel about it. Imitate Tasker's techniques: physical descriptions, analogies, punctuation, metaphors, comparisons, and appeals to the senses.

My Thinking Process

When I wrote this response, I tried to imitate Tasker's technique without copying his aggressive tone exactly. Tasker makes fast food feel dramatic through exaggeration, contrast, and unexpected comparisons. I wanted to use a similar strategy, but in the opposite emotional direction: instead of making the food feel horrifying, I made the bowl feel quiet, balanced, and almost ceremonial.

My main focus was not listing ingredients, but controlling how the reader feels about them. I used contrast, personification, repeated sentence patterns, and abstract language to make the food seem calm and meaningful. This helped me understand that description is not only about what something looks or tastes like; it is also about the mood a writer builds around it.

On the Honey Chicken Super Bowl

At first glance, the bowl offers no spectacle. It does not glitter, does not threaten, does not even persuade. Its surface: grains, vegetables, a halved egg, rests in a kind of quiet arrangement, as if assembled not for display but for recognition. One could almost overlook it.

That would be a mistake.

For upon the first bite, there arrives not shock, but something rarer: a coherence. Not an assault upon the senses, but a revelation of order. The honeyed chicken does not impose its sweetness; it extends it, gently, as though aware of its own limits. Beneath it, the Bolognese answers: not in opposition, but in grounding, drawing the sweetness downward into something more durable, more earthly. There is, in this, a kind of balance that resists spectacle. The avocado, in its quiet green authority, refuses urgency. It does not compete. It absorbs. It slows the palate, insisting that the experience unfold at a human pace rather than a mechanical one. Tomatoes and corn intervene not as decoration, but as intervals, preventing the whole from collapsing into excess.

One does not consume such a bowl so much as participate in it.

There is no domination here, no overwhelming force to surrender to. Instead, there is a quiet insistence: that nourishment need not arrive as violence, that satisfaction may be constructed rather than imposed. In an age of excess, this feels almost improbable. It is best, perhaps, to encounter it without distraction. Not out of ritual obligation, but out of respect for its restraint. In its presence, one is not tested, nor punished, but returned briefly, unexpectedly, to a state in which things make sense.

And that, more than flavor, is what lingers.

Cookbook 1

WR40 · Cookbook Reflection Ann Ge · Apr 3

Cookbook 1

One thing from this course that genuinely surprised me is how much language can shape the way we experience something as ordinary as food. Before this class, I always thought food writing was mostly descriptive, just listing flavors or ingredients. But through the readings and annotations, I started to notice that description is never neutral. Every word choice carries a kind of intention, whether it is to romanticize, critique, or even subtly manipulate the reader's perception.

What I found especially interesting is how tone and structure work together. Some writers exaggerate details to create irony, while others slow down a moment to make something simple feel almost emotional. I did not realize before that even something like the order of sentences can change how a dish is perceived.

This changed the way I read and write. Now when I describe food, I am more aware that I am not just describing it, I am constructing a mood and guiding the reader's response. That shift in awareness is probably the most valuable thing I have learned so far.
