This section focuses on how visual media shapes food meaning. Instead of only asking what the food is, I looked at how image, framing, sound, scale, color, and performance tell the audience how to feel about the food.

My Thinking Process

The screenshot reminded me that visual media already makes an argument before any written analysis begins. Tiny Chef makes food feel miniature, handmade, and playful, while Bourdain's mapo tofu clip uses documentary style and real cooking space to create authority, appetite, and cultural context.

Visual Media Note

Tiny Chef: Scale and Cuteness

"Royal Basil"

The tiny kitchen, puppet body, and oversized basil leaf make the food feel gentle and imaginative. The small scale turns cooking into performance, so the audience watches for emotion and charm as much as for information.

Visual Media Note

Bourdain: Documentary Authority

"Mapo Tofu"

Bourdain's clip presents food through a more realistic visual style. The dish appears in a working kitchen, which makes the food feel connected to place, expertise, and lived culture.

Comparison

Different Kinds of Appetite

playful vs. documentary

Tiny Chef creates appetite through cuteness and fantasy, while Bourdain creates appetite through realism and authority. Both clips make food meaningful, but they use different visual strategies to invite the audience in.

Argument

Images as Claims

visual choices

The main lesson is that an image does not simply decorate a food argument. It tells the audience what kind of attention the food deserves: playful attention, respectful attention, hungry attention, or critical attention.

Feast One

All the while the courses came and went. A thick soup of barley and venison. Salads of sweetgrass and spinach and plums, sprinkled with crushed nuts. Snails in honey and garlic. Sansa had never eaten snails before; Joffrey showed her how to get the snail out of the shell, and fed her the first sweet morsel himself. Then came trout fresh from the river, baked in clay; her prince helped her crack open the hard casing to expose the flaky white flesh within. And when the meat course was brought out, he served her himself, slicing a queen's portion from the joint, smiling as he laid it on her plate.

Marcus and Friends

Exquisite food vibe: exotic but also strange and new. Key details involve Joffrey teaching Sansa how to crack open the snails and how to eat them. Sansa tries them for the first time and is also served a large portion of meat by Joffrey.

Joffrey is royal, yet Sansa is implied to be a commoner or foreigner through her lack of knowledge of the exquisite food and the fact that she is not serving herself. The scene feels very French and medieval-esque with the mention of a prince, queen, and king.

Feast Two

The Lord of White Harbor had furnished the food and drink, black stout and yellow beer and wines red and gold and purple, brought up from the warm southron fat-bottomed ships and aged in his deep cellars. The wedding guests gorged on cod cakes and winter squash, hills of neeps and great round wheels of cheese, on smoking slabs of mutton and beef ribs charred almost black, and lastly on three great wedding pies, as wide across as wagon wheels, their flaky crusts stuffed to bursting with carrots, onions, turnips, parsnips, mushrooms, and chunks of seasoned pork swimming in a savory brown gravy.

Marcus and Friends

The food was very medieval-like. Vibe: wild demons, described like vikings. The Lord of White Harbor presents various foods from medieval time to his guests for the wedding.

Compared to the other feasts, this one is probably the plainest. The sentences are very lengthy, with lists and commas to portray the sheer abundance of food. There is a lot of food, but the quality is not as much of a wow-factor compared to the other feasts.

Feast Three

Of food there was plenty. The war had not touched the fabled bounty of Highgarden. While singers sang and tumblers tumbled, they began with pears poached in wine, and went on to tiny savory fish rolled in salt and cooked crisp, and capons stuffed with onions and mushrooms. There were great loaves of brown bread, mounds of turnips and sweetcorn and pease, immense hams and roast geese and trenchers dripping full of venison stewed with beer and barley.

For the sweet, Lord Caswell's servants brought down trays of pastries from his castle kitchens, cream swans and spun-sugar unicorns, lemon cakes in the shape of roses, spiced honey biscuits and blackberry tarts, apple crisps and wheels of buttery cheese.

Marcus and Friends

Vibe: very cheery and celebrating, with a surplus of food. They have a lot of resources and super fancy desserts, so there is no shortage.

The scene presents entertainment during war and talks about privileged people. It directly states that there is a surplus of food.

Cookbook

WR40 · Cookbook Reflection Ann Ge · Week 03

Week 03 Cookbook

One thing from this course that actually surprised me is how much meaning can come from something that looks like "just description." Before this week, when I read scenes like the three feasts, I mostly focused on what was happening or just the general vibe. If there was a lot of food, I would just think, okay, this is fancy or this is a big event. I didn't really question how the description itself was doing anything beyond setting the scene.

After doing the close reading, that completely changed. All three feasts have a lot of food, but they feel very different, and that difference is not random. In the first feast, everything feels very controlled and structured. The food is fancy and detailed, but the interaction between Sansa and Joffrey is kind of uncomfortable. She doesn't know how to eat the food, and he has to show her, even feed her. That moment made the whole scene feel less like luxury and more like power and control.

The second feast feels almost the opposite. The food is still a lot, but it's more basic, heavier, and described in long, crowded sentences. It feels overwhelming, like there's just too much of everything. The word choices and the long lists make it feel messy instead of elegant. It's still abundance, but a different kind.

The third feast feels the most "perfect," but also the most artificial. There are all these elaborate desserts and decorations, and the text even mentions that the war hasn't affected this place. That detail makes the whole thing feel a little off. It's like the feast is trying to ignore reality and just show off how much they have.

What I didn't expect is that all of this comes from small choices, like what details are included, how sentences are structured, and the order things are described. It made me realize that description is not neutral at all. It's actually doing a lot of work in shaping how we read the scene. After this, I feel like I can't read the same way anymore, because now I keep noticing these details that I used to ignore.
